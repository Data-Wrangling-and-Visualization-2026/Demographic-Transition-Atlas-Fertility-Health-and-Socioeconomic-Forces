Global Patterns of Fertility and Development

This is more than just a map.

Over the past 55 years, fertility has been steadily declining across the world. This shift is linked to changing life priorities, economic pressures, and urbanization. Demographic decline is a serious issue because it accelerates population ageing, reduces labor supply, slows economic growth, and increases pressure on pension systems.

We examined the factors shaping demographic change and identified several patterns that you can explore on this site.

Why adolescent fertility stays so high in many Sub-Saharan African settings

The pattern is not caused by one factor alone. It is usually shaped by several structural pressures acting at the same time:

  • Early and forced marriage. Some girls are married off in childhood, often for money or to reduce the number of mouths to feed.
  • Poverty and lack of schooling. When girls are pushed out of school, motherhood may become the only path left, and poorer households often marry daughters earlier.
  • Limited access to contraception. Even when a girl does not want to become pregnant, contraception may be too expensive, too stigmatized, or blocked by family permission rules.
  • Sexual violence. Many pregnancies among the youngest girls are linked to rape, coercion, or exploitation rather than voluntary relationships.
  • Cultural traditions. In some communities, social norms and ritual practices still encourage an early sexual debut, including harmful "cleansing" practices.

Women's work and fertility in MENA, Afghanistan & Pakistan

Bars show average female labor force participation by region in the latest available year, while the line shows regional TFR.

Why women's labor force participation can stay low in parts of this region

In many settings across the region, low participation is shaped by a mix of social, legal, and material constraints:

  • Social norms and patriarchy. Traditional expectations can confine women to the home and childcare, and in some settings paid work still depends on approval from a husband or father.
  • Taliban rule in Afghanistan. After returning to power in 2021, the Taliban sharply restricted women's access to work and cut them off from most secondary and higher education.
  • Early marriage and high fertility. Girls may marry young and have many children early, leaving little time for study, training, or paid employment.
  • Legal restrictions. In some countries, women still face major limits on travel, passports, or opening a business without a male guardian.
  • Weak family infrastructure. Limited childcare, short leave policies, and unsafe transport can make it nearly impossible to combine paid work with family responsibilities.

    Why child dependency and fertility move together so strongly

    We see a strong link between child dependency and fertility for a simple reason: children are pure dependents. The only way to have many children in a population is to give birth to many children. And the only way to keep child dependency low is to have relatively few births.

    This is not a statistical artifact. It is a demographic law. If a society has high fertility, it will also carry a high child dependency burden. If fertility falls, child dependency falls with it. In plain terms: the more children a population has, the more children it must support.

    *Child dependency ratio: number of children (ages 0-14) per 100 working-age people (ages 15-64).
    *Total dependency ratio: number of dependents (ages 0-14 and 65+) per 100 working-age people (ages 15-64).

    How to interpret indicators

    Measurement units are always shown near each metric, in tooltips, and in chart labels. This helps avoid confusion between levels and rates of change.

    births per woman - average number of births per woman.

    Adolescent fertility

    current US$ - GDP per person in current U.S. dollars.
